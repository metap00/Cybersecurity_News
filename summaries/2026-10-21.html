Qilin and INC Ransomware Groups Exploit Palo Alto GlobalProtect Flaw to Bypass Authentication
🔍 What happened: Two active ransomware operations — Qilin and INC — are exploiting CVE-2026-0257, a critical authentication bypass in Palo Alto Networks PAN-OS GlobalProtect, to forge VPN sessions and gain unauthenticated network access.
👤 Who's affected: Organizations running Palo Alto Networks GlobalProtect VPN portals and gateways with authentication override cookies enabled, particularly those who have not yet applied the May 2026 patch.
✅ What to do: Immediately apply the PAN-OS patch released May 13, 2026; disable authentication override cookies if not required; and hunt for unauthorized VPN sessions in GlobalProtect logs since May.
🎯 ATT&CK: [Initial Access] T1133 · External Remote Services
Both ransomware groups exploit the GlobalProtect authentication flaw to establish what appear to be fully legitimate VPN sessions — using the organization's own remote access infrastructure as their entry point into the network.
▶ Full analysis & beginner explanation

CVE-2026-0257 is an authentication bypass in Palo Alto Networks' GlobalProtect VPN portal and gateway. When authentication override cookies are enabled with specific X.509 certificate configurations, an unauthenticated remote attacker can forge a valid session token and obtain a fully authenticated VPN connection without supplying credentials. Palo Alto issued a patch on May 13 and CISA added the flaw to its KEV catalog on May 29, ordering federal agencies to remediate within three days.

Arctic Wolf Labs first reported Qilin (also known as Agenda) exploiting this flaw in June 2026. October reporting now confirms INC ransomware affiliates are also leveraging it, widening the threat landscape. Both groups operate under a Ransomware-as-a-Service model, meaning numerous independent affiliates use the same toolkits to attack organizations in healthcare, education, manufacturing, and critical infrastructure. Post-exploitation activity ranges from rapid file encryption to full double-extortion — stealing data before encrypting and threatening to publish it.

Organizations that have not patched remain at significant risk. Because attackers establish sessions that appear indistinguishable from legitimate remote-access connections, standard authentication event monitoring may fail to raise alarms. Defenders should examine VPN session logs for connections from unusual IP geolocation clusters or at unusual hours since May.

🎓 For beginners: Think of GlobalProtect as the secure tunnel your remote employees use to connect to the office network from home. This flaw lets attackers trick the tunnel's front door into thinking they already have a valid key — no password needed. Once inside, they can move through the network and eventually lock all your files for ransom.
CVE-2026-0257 · CVSS: 9.3/10 — Critical authentication bypass in PAN-OS GlobalProtect portal/gateway; actively exploited by multiple ransomware groups; patched May 13, 2026.

Hackers Exploit Zimbra Zero-Click Flaw (CVE-2026-73570) to Take Full Control of Email Servers
🔍 What happened: Microsoft Threat Intelligence is tracking active exploitation of CVE-2026-73570, a zero-click vulnerability in Zimbra Collaboration Suite that lets attackers compromise mail servers simply by sending a specially crafted email — no user interaction required.
👤 Who's affected: Organizations globally running internet-facing Zimbra mail servers across multiple industries and regions, confirmed by Microsoft as actively under targeted and opportunistic attack.
✅ What to do: Apply Zimbra security patches immediately; restrict external access to the Zimbra admin interface; and audit server logs for unauthorized webshells, unexpected process executions, and credential-dumping activity.
🎯 ATT&CK: [Persistence] T1505.003 · Server Software Component: Web Shell
After triggering remote code execution via the zero-click flaw, attackers install webshells on the Zimbra server to maintain persistent, interactive access — allowing them to re-enter the system even after credential resets or server reboots.
▶ Full analysis & beginner explanation

CVE-2026-73570 is a zero-click vulnerability in Zimbra Collaboration Suite, a widely deployed enterprise email and collaboration platform. The flaw exists in the way Zimbra parses certain incoming message components; a specially crafted email triggers code execution on the server automatically when the message is processed — the mail administrator does not need to open, preview, or interact with the message in any way.

Microsoft Threat Intelligence observed post-exploitation behavior including webshell deployment, local privilege escalation, collection of authentication secrets (Kerberos tickets and NTLM hashes), and staging of mailbox data for exfiltration. The breadth of affected organizations — spanning multiple regions and industries — suggests both opportunistic scanning and targeted campaigns may be leveraging the same flaw, possibly indicating multiple threat actors exploiting a recently shared proof-of-concept.

Defenders should treat any Zimbra server exposed to the internet as potentially compromised until patched. Beyond applying updates, incident responders recommend checking for unexpected files in the Zimbra webroot directory, unusual outbound connections, and newly created administrative accounts with unfamiliar names or email addresses.

🎓 For beginners: Imagine if receiving a letter in your mailbox caused your house to automatically unlock — that's what a zero-click exploit does. The attacker just sends an email; Zimbra processes it, and in doing so accidentally runs the attacker's code on the server. They then leave a hidden backdoor (webshell) so they can come back anytime and access all your organization's email.

▶ Full analysis & beginner explanation

Zscaler ThreatLabz's latest ransomware report documents a significant operational shift: ransomware affiliates are deploying AI-powered tools during the post-breach dwell phase to automate what was previously a slow, manual process of finding valuable data. Rather than blindly encrypting all files or extracting everything, attackers now use local language models and classification scripts to rapidly identify merger documents, employee PII, patient records, and financial reports — the files with maximum extortion leverage.

This changes the extortion dynamic. Traditional double-extortion relied on threatening to release whatever the attacker happened to grab. Zscaler notes that dwell time between initial access and ransomware deployment is shrinking as a result, since less time is needed to manually identify high-value data. Defenders should prioritize monitoring for bulk file access patterns that deviate from normal employee behavior, especially on file servers, SharePoint repositories, and backup systems.

🎓 For beginners: Normally a ransomware attacker is like a burglar who grabs random items — they take whatever's there. AI changes this to a sophisticated art thief who quickly scans your home, identifies the most valuable paintings, and only takes those. Instead of "pay or we dump your data," the threat becomes "pay or we publish your CEO's salary negotiations and your top 1,000 customer records" — far more credible and damaging.

💡 Today I Learned: Zero-Click Exploits ▶
A zero-click exploit is a cyberattack that requires absolutely no interaction from the victim — there's nothing to click, open, approve, or respond to. Attackers send a specially crafted message, packet, or file that triggers a vulnerability in the target application automatically when it is received or processed. The Zimbra flaw in today's news is a prime example: simply receiving a malicious email caused the mail server to execute attacker code, with no administrator ever reading or opening the message — making these attacks especially dangerous because even the most security-aware users cannot protect themselves through caution alone.
📖 Glossary: Zero-Click · Webshell · PAN-OS · CVSS · Exfiltration · TTP ▶
Zero-Click — An attack that requires no user interaction; the malicious payload executes automatically when the target application receives or processes a specially crafted input, with no click, tap, or open needed.
Webshell — A malicious script uploaded to a compromised web or application server that gives attackers a persistent, browser-accessible backdoor to execute commands remotely even after credentials are changed.
PAN-OS — Palo Alto Networks' proprietary operating system that powers its firewalls, VPN gateways (GlobalProtect), and network security appliances; the platform affected by CVE-2026-0257.
CVSS — Common Vulnerability Scoring System; a 0–10 numeric scale rating software vulnerability severity: 0–3.9 Low, 4.0–6.9 Medium, 7.0–8.9 High, 9.0–10.0 Critical.
Exfiltration — The unauthorized transfer of data out of a compromised environment to attacker-controlled servers or storage; typically the final stage of a breach before a ransom demand or data leak threat.
TTP — Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures; the behavioral patterns characterizing how a specific threat actor operates, as catalogued in frameworks like MITRE ATT&CK; knowing an actor's TTPs helps defenders predict and block their next moves.
