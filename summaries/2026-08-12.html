Hackers Shut Down Steam Turbine at Polish Heating Plant via Private Cellular Network
🔍 What happened: Attackers scanned a private APN (cellular network), found a Wago PLC with SSH exposed, and used it to shut down a steam turbine and water treatment system at a Polish combined heat and power plant serving 50,000 residents.
👤 Who's affected: The CHP plant operator and approximately 50,000 residents it heats; the incident was part of a coordinated campaign that simultaneously struck 30 Polish renewable energy facilities and another CHP plant on the same day.
✅ What to do: Audit all private APN connections for OT network exposure, disable SSH on any PLC reachable from a cellular interface, enforce strict network segmentation between IT and OT environments, and consult CISA's ICS security guidance immediately.
🎯 ATT&CK: [Initial Access] T1599 · Network Boundary Bridging
Attackers pivoted from the private cellular APN network into the plant's OT segment by exploiting an SSH service on a Wago PLC, crossing the IT/OT boundary without traversing traditional IT infrastructure — the first known use of a private APN as an OT attack vector.
▶ Full analysis & beginner explanation

This attack on a Polish combined heat and power (CHP) plant is a landmark event in ICS/OT security: investigators describe it as the first known case of attackers using a private APN as the entry point into an industrial operational technology environment. A private APN is a dedicated mobile data network — typically set up by industrial operators so equipment can be remotely monitored without routing traffic over the public internet. The security assumption was that a private APN was inherently isolated. Attackers disproved that assumption by systematically scanning the APN's IP address space, identifying a Wago programmable logic controller with an SSH service listening, and using that foothold to reach and manipulate the plant's operational systems.

Once inside, the attackers shut down a steam turbine and the process-water treatment system, disrupting the plant's cogeneration cycle. The incident occurred on the same day as coordinated attacks against 30 other Polish renewable energy facilities and a second CHP plant, suggesting a deliberate, multi-target campaign likely orchestrated by a well-resourced threat actor. Plant operators initially attributed the shutdown to contractor error before forensic analysis revealed the true cause. Recovery operations began while attackers were still active in the network, and thankfully customers lost neither heat nor electricity.

The security lesson is profound: any network-connected device in an OT environment — even one on a "private" cellular APN — must be treated as a potential attack surface. SSH services should never be enabled on PLCs facing any network without strict access controls, authentication, and logging.

🎓 For beginners: A power plant in Poland was hacked through its private mobile phone network. The plant connected some of its industrial machines (called PLCs — programmable logic controllers) to a private cellular network for remote monitoring. The attackers found one of those machines had an open remote-access port (SSH) and used it to break in and shut down a key turbine. Think of it like a burglar discovering a secret back door into a building that the owners assumed nobody knew about — because it was on a "private" street.

▶ Full analysis & beginner explanation

Microsoft's August 2026 Patch Tuesday is one of the largest in company history, patching over 400 CVEs across Windows, Office, Azure, and other products. The critical zero-day is CVE-2026-68820 — a use-after-free vulnerability in afd.sys, the kernel-mode Windows Ancillary Function Driver that handles WinSock (Windows Sockets) networking. A use-after-free bug occurs when software continues to reference memory after it has been freed; an attacker can manipulate that freed memory region to redirect program execution, typically achieving arbitrary code execution at a higher privilege level.

CVE-2026-68820 is a local privilege escalation (LPE), meaning an attacker must already have limited access to the machine before exploiting it. This makes it a classic second-stage exploit: pair initial access (via phishing, a web exploit, or stolen credentials) with CVE-2026-68820 and an attacker achieves full SYSTEM-level control. Microsoft confirms active exploitation in the wild. Additionally, two other zero-days were publicly disclosed (potentially giving attackers a head-start before patches land): one in Remote Desktop and one affecting a Windows network protocol stack component. The 37 Critical RCE flaws this cycle are especially alarming — multiple affect Hyper-V (virtualization), meaning a successful exploit could escape a virtual machine and compromise the host server.

Other vendors with coordinated August patches include SAP (CVSS 10.0 vuln), Adobe, Cisco, and TP-Link (15 Omada flaws).

This Sandworm campaign demonstrates that Russia's GRU, rather than always deploying sophisticated technical exploits, invests heavily in multi-step social engineering to achieve persistence inside Ukrainian networks. DeadLock Ransomware Stores Command Infrastructure on Polygon Blockchain, Making Takedowns Effectively Impossible
🔍 What happened: Microsoft Threat Intelligence published a detailed breakdown of DeadLock, a Rust-based ransomware group with 80+ victims that stores its C2 server configuration in Polygon smart contracts on the public blockchain — combined with the Session decentralized messaging network and Wasabi cloud storage — making its command infrastructure impossible to seize through traditional law-enforcement methods.
👤 Who's affected: 80+ organizations globally with over half in Europe; sectors include manufacturing, professional services, and technology; the blockchain-based infrastructure means disruption of operations requires stopping the attacker, not the infrastructure.
✅ What to do: Focus on preventive defenses rather than hoping for infrastructure takedowns: implement immutable offline backups, deploy behavioral ransomware detection, block known DeadLock indicators of compromise, and ensure rapid incident response plans are tested.
🎯 ATT&CK: [Command and Control] T1568 · Dynamic Resolution
DeadLock stores C2 server addresses in Polygon smart contracts, enabling dynamic updates to its command infrastructure without any server to seize; since blockchain data is immutable and globally distributed, defenders cannot disrupt the C2 "dead drop" through traditional takedown methods — only blocking access to Polygon entirely would work.
▶ Full analysis & beginner explanation

DeadLock represents a significant evolution in ransomware infrastructure design. Traditional ransomware gangs rely on web servers or "bulletproof" hosting for their command-and-control (C2) communications — infrastructure that law enforcement can seize, disrupt, or poison with sinkhole operations. DeadLock's operators have engineered around this limitation by storing their C2 configuration in Polygon smart contracts. Polygon is an Ethereum-compatible public blockchain; like all blockchain data, these smart contract entries are permanent, globally replicated, and cannot be deleted or modified — even by Polygon itself.

The malware reads its current C2 server address from the blockchain (updating dynamically if the operators push new addresses), establishes encrypted command communications through the Session decentralized messaging network (which uses three-hop onion routing and requires no identifying information to create an account), and stores exfiltrated victim data in Wasabi cloud object storage. Microsoft's detailed technical analysis of the Rust-based encryptor reveals it is carefully engineered to avoid crashing victim systems — it deliberately skips certain system directories so the operating system remains functional enough for the victim to negotiate a ransom payment. Files are encrypted with AES-256. The group has claimed 80+ victims since mid-2025, with European organizations comprising the majority.

🎓 For beginners: Most ransomware gangs run regular web servers that governments can shut down, cutting off the hackers' control. DeadLock is smarter — it hides its "home base" on a blockchain (the same technology that powers cryptocurrency). Since blockchains can't be deleted or taken offline by any single authority, even if police wanted to disrupt DeadLock's operations, there's no server to seize. It's like a criminal gang replacing their headquarters with a permanent message engraved in stone that nobody can erase. 💡 Today I Learned: Private APN Attack Vector ▶
A private APN (Access Point Name) is a dedicated mobile data network that enterprises set up so their industrial equipment can communicate over cellular networks without touching the public internet — the assumption being that "private" means "secure." Today's Polish power plant attack demonstrated this assumption is dangerously wrong: if a device on the private APN has an open service like SSH, an attacker who gains access to the APN's IP space (through a compromised SIM, a rogue device, or poor APN security controls) can reach and attack industrial equipment just as easily as if it were on the internet. The fix is to treat every device on a private APN as if it faces the internet: disable unnecessary services, enforce authentication, and segment OT devices from any network-reachable segment.
📖 Glossary: APN · OT · PLC · Patch Tuesday · MSP · KEV · ZTP ▶
APN — Access Point Name; a private cellular network gateway that enterprises configure to connect OT/IoT devices to mobile networks without routing traffic over the public internet, though improperly secured APNs can still be an attack entry point.
OT — Operational Technology; hardware and software that monitors and controls physical processes in industrial environments (power plants, water treatment, manufacturing), as opposed to IT systems that process information.
PLC — Programmable Logic Controller; a ruggedized industrial computer used to automate machinery and processes in factories, power plants, and utilities; increasingly a target for cyber attacks as they become network-connected.
Patch Tuesday — Microsoft's practice of releasing security patches on the second Tuesday of each month; many other vendors (Adobe, SAP, Cisco) coordinate their own patch releases on the same day to simplify enterprise patch management cycles.
MSP — Managed Service Provider; a company that remotely manages a customer's IT infrastructure and end-user systems, typically using platforms like N-able N-central; MSP compromise is high-value for attackers because it provides access to all of an MSP's downstream customers simultaneously.
KEV — Known Exploited Vulnerabilities; CISA's authoritative catalog of CVEs confirmed to be actively exploited in the wild; US federal agencies are required to patch KEV-listed vulnerabilities within specified timeframes, and all organizations should treat KEV additions as high-priority patch signals.
ZTP — Zero-Touch Provisioning; a method for automatically configuring network devices (routers, switches, access points) without manual intervention, using pre-defined templates and cloud-based onboarding; the TP-Link Omada vulnerabilities this month targeted flaws in ZTP workflows that allowed device hijacking during the provisioning process.
